## Key Takeaways

Lucy Williams-Jones has qualified for President's Club 25 times in a row — over two and a half decades of consistency across BMC, MongoDB, DataDog, and now Astronomer. Her formula is anything but linear: she argues that the modern buying committee (10–15 people, with security, finance, DPA/paperwork that "was not a thing 25 years ago") has made the old one-person champion → economic-buyer model obsolete. The episode introduces the **champion spectrum** (champion ↔ coach ↔ competitor's champion) as a diagnostic, and stresses that both building champions and earning economic-buyer access come down to discovery done well.

> 1. **Buying committees have exploded** — from one or two IT sign-offs to 10–15 people, so you need multiple champions across lines of business, and an "EB" is rarely a single ultimate boss — there are often "seven or eight ultimate EBs."
> 2. **The champion spectrum** — champion at one end, competitor's champion at the other, coach in the middle. A coach helps but "will not risk their reputation for you"; a champion will vouch for you and risk their own neck.
> 3. **Business vs. personal vested interest is what moves people** — find both for each champion (promotion, time back with the family, avoiding sleepless nights), and the champion will want to introduce you to the EB because it's in *their* interest.
> 4. **An EB's best use is leverage and influence, not day-to-day work** — and an EB as your *only* champion makes you lazy, because you don't build champions at the lower levels.
> 5. **Everything comes down to discovery done well** — which is preparation + point of view + the business outcomes. "The most efficient path to a closed deal is the longest way around."

## Guest Background

## Core Thesis

The old-fashioned picture of one economic buyer and one champion no longer works. Buying committees have grown to 10–15 people, so a seller must **build a champion infrastructure** — multiple champions at multiple levels, across lines of business, each with a discovered personal and professional vested interest. The **champion spectrum** is a diagnostic tool for seeing where each contact sits (champion / coach / competitor's champion) and for driving action: convert coaches and pull competitor champions through discovery and proven value. Economic buyers are best used for leverage and influence — removing blockers, accelerating decisions, helping in negotiation — not as a crutch that lets you avoid building the lower-level champion infrastructure.

## Major Topics Discussed

### [[00:00:18]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=18) What's changed in 25 years: AI, lazy sellers, and data-rich buyers

Lucy starts with culture: "the biggest change I've seen over the last 25 years is the use of AI," with tools like Groove (email automation) and ChatGPT taking away the manual tasks sellers used to do. Her worry is that AI "is actually probably making sales people a little bit lazy." The fix is a balance — AI as "a junior analyst that you've now got working for you" for research, but you must still read and form your own **point of view**, because "the magic happens" in that moment of contemplating what a goal or challenge means — and that point of view is what carries credibility and gets you introduced up to the economic buyer. She'd take **eight A players using zero AI** over B players using as much AI as they want. Andy adds that buyers now have as much data as sellers — they arrive knowing who they want to choose and with pricing questions, and a problem buyer goes to six vendors at once, not one.

### [[00:15:58]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=958) Buying committees, the "ultimate EB," and marketplace champions

Buying committees have "grown and grown": before it was one or two people in IT signing off even a $100K+ order — now it's 10–15 people, plus paperwork, roadblocks, finance, security, DPAs, "which was not a thing 25 years ago" (you used to tick "yes" on a shrink-wrapped MSA). Lucy hates the term "EB/ultimate EB": "how many times have I had my team say 'who's the EB?' … but there's actually someone else who can say no." In practice there are **seven or eight ultimate EBs across the lines of business.** MEDDIC must be treated as an open persona rather than one person per role. You also need the **cloud-marketplace champion** — the person whose job it is to put the order through the marketplace and accept it; if that admin person goes on holiday at the 11th hour, your deal slips. You can't overlook anyone in the sales process.

### [[00:19:37]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=1177) Champions and the "change at the guard"

Competitive deals are often lost not with the EB but by an unforeseen angle. Because people move every ~3 years and bring their "die-hard tools" with them, a new guard can turn your customer's wall-to-wall footprint into a competitor's win — an RFP can even be written for the competitor ("which is why you should never answer a blind RFP unless you've influenced the decision criteria"). So get to EBs early and make them your champion, before the change at the guard — January is a great time to get back in front of EBs to learn their priorities for the new year.

### [[00:22:41]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=1361) Planning-stage advice and the SCIPAB framework for EB outreach

For planning, Lucy says start with **strategic intent, not activity** — don't blast a C-level with irrelevant content. Do the research on what they're trying to achieve that fiscal and what went wrong last year, and go in with a point of view on the market and trends. Her team's email framework is **SCIPAB**: Situation, Complication, Implication (the first three paragraphs, always in that order), then Position, Action, Benefit (PAB can be reordered), and it always ends with an *ask* — a meeting, a product view, or a referral to a more qualified stakeholder. Write a strong point-of-view email yourself ("the idea is not to put it through ChatGPT"). If you have no relationship with the EB, find a **champion to take you up or across** — a great champion test.

### [[00:25:05]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=1505) Rethinking the definition of a champion

Andy pushes back on a common definition ("a champion introduces you to the economic buyer") — it's "so binary" and not really a champion trait. The real sign is being seen as **so valuable and such a great partner that the champion *wants* to get you in front of the EB because it's in their own interest** — once you've aligned your value to their vested interest, they'll do the selling internally for you. Lucy adds: "a champion is a champion because they have access and are well respected… but they're going to have their own win. Why are they doing it?" Find what's in it for them early — promotion, weekends with the kids — and work together in partnership.

### [[00:27:28]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=1648) Champion traits: power & influence, and champion vs. coach

For a champion, **power and influence is the non-negotiable trait** — "doesn't matter how strong their vested interest is… if they haven't got the influence to capture the person's attention," it doesn't go anywhere. A vocal contact without access is a **coach** (still valuable). Consistent top performers are distinguished by being "the fastest to identify things like 'I do not have a champion, I just have a coach'" and having the confidence to either go qualify out or go find a champion while managing the coach. Champion traits Lucy watches for: someone who is **not always available** (if they're always on WhatsApp/text, "probably question whether they're busy enough to be a champion"), someone always thinking about strategic intent and how the solution helps them, and someone who takes **your stance in front of others** and sells on your behalf.

### [[00:33:01]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=1981) The five gold coins, rules of engagement, and trusting your champion

Lucy tells her teams to treat a champion as having **five gold coins** — spend them sparingly, at the point of inflection where you'll get the most benefit, "don't just use them willy-nilly because the champions will soon disappear." Andy adds the trap of treating a champion as a binary event, getting one, and then admiring them ("show them off to my manager") without ever asking anything of them — you have to actually get them executing. Lucy's build cycle is **identification → build → test → use**, and the coins apply to the *use* phase. On communication she's wary of **WhatsApp**: it's too easy, doesn't prove a strong relationship, and she'd rather have a call then a confirmatory follow-up email. Andy suggests setting **rules of engagement** with a champion ("how to work with me") and trusting your champions 100% — with good champion + EB access you shouldn't be chasing for updates; constant chasing is the micro version of a destructive micro-managing boss.

### [[00:41:20]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=2480) EB engagement: the best use of an EB is leverage and influence

EB engagement is "probably the hardest bit of the sales cycle." Lucy's org uses PG (probably "point/principal") Fridays to reach stakeholders the team can't get to; getting EB access is a **team effort**, because not all EBs want to speak to a salesperson unless you lead with value. Andy is "a massive fan of delegating to the peer" and of making **no ask on the first engagement**, done *early* — before the first meeting even. Have your EB's peer reach out to say they'll be the executive sponsor and to open the line of communication, with no ask yet — so the first thing the EB hears from you isn't "don't kill this project" at the 11th hour, but that you've warmed them up. In MEDDICC playbook, **EB access comes before POV** — meet the budget holder to ensure they're comfortable with the process before both organizations invest in a trial and business case — because a POV run without that runs the risk of "just die[ing] a death."

### [[00:48:19]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=2899) The champion spectrum introduced

The hosts name the framework they previewed — **the champion spectrum**: champion at one end, competitor's champion at the other, coach in the middle. The question it answers: how do you pull someone from being a competitor's champion (or a coach) back toward being *your* champion? The answer is via discovery — specifically the **business outcomes** part — because that's where you find vested interest, and because the coach/champion distinction is "the willingness to risk their own reputation for you." The champion spectrum is "a really great diagnostic tool" that forces you to build out **champion infrastructure at every level** — you can and should have multiple champions across lines of business, and an EB as your only champion makes you lazy because you never build that infrastructure.

### [[00:53:14]](https://youtu.be/5sPRVuMNNeM?t=3194) Champion outputs, pulling competitor champions, and the theme of the episode

Asked what a champion executing looks like, Lucy lists the outputs — framed as **"a menu, not a checklist"**: selling your solution internally without you there; getting you the opportunity to present to the EB or other stakeholders; introducing you to new people; giving you intel/insight on the competitor and on how the deal will be scored; and helping you quantify the business outcomes. To pull someone from being a competitor's champion, first **understand what the competitor did to earn that loyalty** (prior relationship, prior history, a huge past win, or a relationship with the competitor's rep), then understand why they're doing it and bring an alternative — built over time, through discovery, never overnight. The theme of the episode, per Andy: **discovery done well = preparation + point of view + the business outcomes**, and "if you get those three things right, discovery's done well and everything else follows." He closes with the quote: **"The most efficient path to a closed deal is the longest way around."**
