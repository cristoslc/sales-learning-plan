# How to Move from Feature Selling to Gap Selling — Keenan

- **Guest:** Keenan, founder of A Sales Guy (sales consulting, recruiting, and training); author of *Gap Selling*
- **Hosts:** Sam Jacobs
- **Podcast:** Sales Hacker Podcast
- **Published:** 2019

## Key Takeaways

Selling has nothing to do with you or your product. It is about the buyer's gap — the space between their untenable current state and their desired future state — and the seller's entire job is to understand and expand that gap. People buy when the gap is big enough; the size of the gap, not the features, determines motivation, price tolerance, and close probability.

> 1. **It's not about you** — nobody cares about you, your product, or your company. Most sales training and most opening pitches ("we're a 500-person company with...") are product-centric noise that prospects experience as a waste of time.
> 2. **The current state must be made *untenable*, not hellish** — and you can't tell the customer their current state (perceptions are involved); you can only ask enough questions to let them uncover the full extent of it themselves.
> 3. **Discovery has five sections, done twice** — for both current state and future state: (1) the physical/literal environment, (2) the problems, (3) the impact of those problems, (4) the emotional state, (5) the root cause (current) / solution employed (future). Walk the buyer through all of it and they "sell themselves."
> 4. **The migraine-pill metaphor** — willingness to pay for the same painkiller goes from $5 to $1,000,000 depending entirely on the gap: a lazy Sunday vs. a $50M proposal due tomorrow vs. a malignant brain tumor at 35 vs. the same tumor at 90 in a nursing home. Same pain, different gap. If you don't understand the size of the gap, "we're not selling, we're taking orders."
> 5. **Command of the problem, not command of the solution, drives credibility** — like a doctor who asks left-field questions and thereby demonstrates mastery of the problem. Once the buyer believes you understand the problem, they believe by default that you can solve it.
> 6. **Problem-centric vs. product-centric selling is a complete inversion** — problem-centric sellers talk business problems, focus on the buyer, ask questions and stay quiet, are motivated by the customer's success, create demand, and control the sale. Product-centric sellers talk features, react to demand, and compete on price.
> 7. **Demos are good — but only after discovery, and only the 4–6 features that map to the buyer's stated problems** — anchor each: "can you see how this keeps that from happening?" Then stop.
> 8. **Closing is a waste of time** — "if you have to close at the end, you didn't do something in the beginning." Buyers close themselves when they've been brought along through current state → future state → gap → solution, with acknowledgments at each step ("micro commitments" / "the next yes").
> 9. **No scripts — teach sellers the business instead** — an oncologist doesn't need a script because they command the disease. Sellers need command of the customer's business, problems, impacts, and challenges, so questions flow from knowledge; a question-script devolves into a 20-deep if-then hierarchy that doesn't survive contact with a real answer.
> 10. **Three competencies separate people who can do this from people who can't** — phenomenal critical thinking, excellent business acumen, and off-the-charts listening with connect-the-dots intuition.

## Core Thesis

The sale is determined by the **gap**: the distance between the buyer's current state and their future state. The bigger and more untenable the current state — as *perceived by the buyer* — the more they'll pay, the more engaged they'll be, and the higher the close probability. Sellers have no influence over the gap when they pitch features, because they never diagnose the current state; that's why they end up "taking orders" and closing with cheesy urgency at the end of a process where they "ran six miles and have to look back and say, okay, you ready?" The cure is problem-centric selling: structured discovery across environment, problems, impact, emotion, and root cause, followed by a demo that shows only the features that map to the problems the buyer themselves articulated.

## Major Topics Discussed

### How Keenan teaches discovery: the five-section structure

For the **current state**: (1) the physical and literal environment — systems, org size, tooling, on-prem vs. cloud, scoped strictly to what you sell ("do not ask questions that have nothing to do with what you sell"); (2) the problems within that situation; (3) the impact of each problem — the missed numbers, the lawsuits, the compliance exposure; (4) the general emotional state — indifference, fear, anger; (5) the root cause, which "acts as my conduit to my service" — if the buyer agrees on the root cause and your product eradicates it, the transition from current to future state is seamless. Then the same five sections for the **future state**: goals and measures of success, which problems go away, the new impact, the desired emotional state, and the solution they'd employ. Take a buyer through that whole process and "they basically sell themselves... they just walked themselves right through the door."

### The migraine-pill metaphor: the gap is the sale

Keenan's training exercise: a blistering migraine — what would you pay for the cure? $5, yes; most hands drop around $500. Then layer in context: tomorrow you present a $50M proposal with a $50K commission, it isn't done, and it can't be done with the headache — now $5–7K. Then: the headache has lasted four months, hurts in one spot, and messes with your motor skills — it's a malignant brain tumor, you die in six months — now a million dollars, every hand up. Then the twist: a 90-year-old in a nursing home with the same tumor — hands back down. Same pain every time; the *gap* is what moved. A seller who doesn't understand the size of the buyer's gap "is not selling... [they're] taking orders because [they] have no influence on the sale whatsoever."

### Why most sellers never do this (the Workday/Salesforce business case)

In a live training, Keenan asked a software-adoption client whether they ever discuss a prospect's growth goals or ask a CIO what the business case was for the Workday or Salesforce investment and whether the return is being met today. None of them had. His point: "if I have invested $120M in implementing a software application, there's a business case... if I'm not getting those productivity returns, I'm losing on the business case. You're telling me you can get me back to those productivity returns — so how in the world is nobody having that conversation with anybody?" It's not intuitive; it requires thinking in the buyer's business context rather than the product's feature context.

### Demos: after diagnosis, and only the relevant features

Keenan loves demos — done right. Do discovery until the buyer says "this is my problem and I want to solve it"; the doctor metaphor explains why: a doctor asking unexpected questions demonstrates command of the problem, which raises your belief they can treat you. "It's not your command of the solution that gets you credibility — it's your command of the problem." Then show only the 4–6 features that map to what the buyer told you, anchored each time ("you told me you were struggling with X — let me show you how we solve that"), and ask for the acknowledgment: "can you see how this is going to do that?"

### Against closing, against scripts

Closing tactics are the residue of product-centric selling — sellers who never brought the buyer along must guess what the buyer thinks at the end. With acknowledgment checkpoints at every stage (current state correct? future state correct? gap correct? solution anchored?), the buyer closes themselves — Keenan has hung up from discovery calls and had the buyer ask "what are the next steps to getting this wrapped up?" On scripts: a script is anything the seller reads or uses to *drive* the conversation — and since selling is not telling, command statements are anti-selling. A script of pure questions would need a 20-deep if-then hierarchy because you can't anticipate answers. The parallel is the oncologist: no script, because they command the disease — its causes, stages, symptoms, triage. The fix is educating sellers on the customer's business until they can "engage them on the fly without a fricking script."
