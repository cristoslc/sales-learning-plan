## Key Takeaways

Negotiation is an emotional-intelligence discipline, not a rational-argument contest. The skills Voss built in FBI hostage negotiation apply equally to business because all human decisions have an emotional component — and they work even when the counterpart knows you're using them.

> 1. **There's far more space between "yes" and "no" than most people realize** — a "no" makes the other side feel protected, and protected people relax and think more clearly [~00:02:00].
> 2. **Never call and ask "do you have a few minutes?"** — ask "is now a bad time to talk?" It either gets you a focused conversation or a scheduled time, with no ambiguity [~00:05:00].
> 3. **The most powerful move after friction is earning a "that's right"** — summarize the situation from *their* point of view, even when it sounds against your interests. "You're right" means "shut up and go away"; "that's right" means an epiphany and collaboration [~00:07:00].
> 4. **People fall into three negotiator types — assertive, accommodator, analytical** — and each reads the same behavior (like silence) completely differently. Impasses are usually type mismatches, not substance disagreements [~00:10:00].
> 5. **Compromise is "a horrible word"** — splitting the difference is lazy and leaves money on the table. Pursue high-value trades instead: give what's cheap for you but valuable to them [~00:20:00].
> 6. **The adversary is the situation, not the person** — partner with the human across the table to solve the shared problem; hammering people creates lifelong enemies who wait to pay you back [~00:21:00].
> 7. **Lying is a bad approach, and so is being a hard bargainer** — both destroy your deal flow once reputation spreads. "No deal is better than a bad deal" [~00:36:00].
> 8. **Let the other side go first** — they've rehearsed their talking points and can't hear you until they've said them; and their opening contains intelligence no research can surface (15 minutes of conversation can replace two weeks of research) [~00:37:00].
> 9. **If someone is talking to you at all, you have leverage** — "it doesn't matter what leverage they have on you; what matters is what they think of the leverage you have on them" [~00:41:00].

## Guest Background

Chris Voss spent 14 years on the New York City Joint Terrorist Task Force, was lead crisis negotiator for the FBI's New York division, and became the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. He was the only agent to talk his way into Harvard Law School's negotiation course, where he collaborated with (and was encouraged by) Sheila Heen, John Richardson, and Doug Stone. After retiring from the Bureau in 2007, he founded the Black Swan Group and authored *Never Split the Difference*. He has taught business negotiation in the MBA programs at USC and Georgetown, taught at Harvard, and guest lectured at Northwestern. He grew up in small-town Iowa to blue-collar parents — a background he returns to with a joke about his mother ("What's the difference between an Iowa mom and a terrorist? You can negotiate with a terrorist").

## Core Thesis

Everything conventionally taught about negotiation is wrong: people are not rational, there's no such thing as "fair," and compromise is the worst outcome. The real craft is **tactical empathy** — understanding and articulating the counterpart's emotions and worldview well enough that they say "that's right" — because people can only collaborate once they feel heard and safe. Every decision has an emotional component ("we make our minds up based on what we care about"), so tools that work on intensely emotional counterparties (hostage-takers) work even better on calmer ones. The skills are subtle enough that even a kidnapping ringleader called his negotiator weeks later to say thanks — negotiation that leaves the other side feeling *better*, not beaten, is the only kind that compounds.

## Major Topics Discussed

### [[00:01:00]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=60) The chairs story: "no" is the beginning of the conversation

The host's opening anecdote — told "no" on director's chairs, then asking a different question ("can I go pick them up?") and getting them delivered — sets up two lessons: there's much more space between yes and no than people assume, and what happens *after* a "no" matters more than the "no" itself. A "no" makes people feel protected; protected people relax, center, and think. Positivity compounds this: your mind works up to 31% more effectively in a positive frame, and pleasant interaction triggers that in the other person.

### [[00:04:50]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=290) After the "no": hesitation, then "that's right"

After a "no," hesitate — give the counterpart space to think (three seconds can be an eternity for mental processing). The next milestone is a "that's right," which you earn by summarizing the situation from the *other* person's point of view — even when the summary sounds against your own interests. Co-writer Tahl Raz calls it "a subtle epiphany"; Voss adds that it signals felt empathy, and people who feel understood want to collaborate. (Contrast: "you're right" is what people say when they want you to stop talking — illustrated by Voss's football-playing son saying "you're right" to his coaches while continuing to play wrong.)

### [[00:07:48]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=468) Three approaches to conflict — and why silence misleads

The world breaks into three negotiator types: **assertive** (being understood matters more than agreement), **accommodator** (relationship continuity matters most), and **analytical** (sharing their reasoning matters most). For each, the deal is secondary to something else. The same silence means totally different things to each: an assertive assumes you want them to keep talking; an analyst is just thinking (and is grateful for the quiet); an accommodator going silent is a danger sign (it's the only time they go silent). Experienced negotiators pick up all three traits — partly because you're six times more likely to make a deal with someone who likes you.

### [[00:13:15]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=795) Into hostage negotiation — via a suicide hotline

Voss's path in: a knee injury moved him off the SWAT team; the New York office head told every applicant "volunteer at a suicide hotline" and exactly two ever did — he was one. Doing what the right person told him to do got him the next FBI crisis negotiation training slot. A rare real bank-robbery-with-hostages in Brooklyn followed, and teaching the incident revealed he liked teaching.

### [[00:16:43]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=1003) The Black Swan Group, and a $10M-to-zero "that's right"

Named for Taleb's black swans — small, innocuous things with massive impact. The flagship story: a long Philippines kidnapping with a $10M ransom demand that went to zero in one five-minute conversation after the government negotiator got the terrorist to say "that's right" on the phone. Weeks later, the same terrorist called the negotiator to say he'd been about to kill the hostage, the phone conversation had stopped him, and "they should promote you." The skills work even when the counterpart recognizes them — "even when they know, they're still OK with it."

### [[00:20:35]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=1235) Compromise is lazy; pursue high-value trades

Splitting the difference leaves money and better outcomes on the table. Voss's line: "never be so sure of what you want that you wouldn't take something better." His magazine-cover example: the Memphis Bar Association needed a cover photo (trivial for them) and he wanted the exposure (valuable and hard for him to get otherwise) — a perfect high-value trade. The adversary is the *situation*; the person across the table is your potential partner in solving it.

### [[00:27:56]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=1676) Unknown unknowns — where Black Swans live

Two people in a negotiation always hold back information — including things they don't know the other side cares about. Deception-detection only covers what people *know* is important; the valuable information emerges from getting conversation flowing. The Christmas-tree anecdote: a student labels his wife's resistance ("it seems like you had real trees growing up"), she opens up about childhood Christmas memories, and he switches sides instantly — the "crazy" position had an unspoken reason that dissolved the conflict once surfaced. Labeling ("it seems like...") is the tool for surfacing these.

### [[00:34:20]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=2060) Emotional entanglement and the accusations audit

The closer you are to someone, the harder it is to articulate their grievance against you. Voss's **accusations audit** — "it seems like I've been a jerk the entire time" — is disarming precisely because it says what the other person is already thinking. His ex-Mrs.-Voss story is the cautionary tale: hotline-trained, he said "you sound angry," and she exploded — because the accurate label was "it seems like I haven't respected you," something she could say "that's right" to. Anger directed *at you*, which you're held responsible for, requires owning the accusation, not just naming the emotion.

### [[00:36:00]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=2160) Against lying and against hard bargaining

Voss doesn't lie in negotiations — the other side always finds out, and they're still in your world afterward. His Harvard colleagues (Bob Mnookin's program) heavily emphasize not lying. His early Harvard simulations used invisible hard-bargaining techniques from kidnapping work — until his reputation spread and counterparties became afraid to even speak ("word got out... no one would talk to me"). The energy-CEO story lands the same lesson: a signed agreement sat unsigned because making any deal with a known hard bargainer reads as "being beaten." Reputation is a deal-flow asset.

### [[00:37:00]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=2220) Start by letting them go first

Everyone arrives rehearsed; they cannot listen until they've had their say. Going first also feeds intelligence: Voss estimates he extracts in 10–15 minutes of conversation what would take two weeks of external research to get — internal pressures, the boss's reaction to the last deal, whether they're even hungry. Some things no research can surface.

### [[00:41:00]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=2460) Leverage exists in the eye of the beholder

"If somebody is talking to you, you've got something they want — period." Jim Camp: there's no such thing as leverage, only its perception. Kidnappers want money and recognition; if their case is public, publicity dynamics (including fear of ridicule, the one x-factor nobody tolerates) give the negotiator behavior-changing levers. Fear of loss is the number-one driver of decisions.

### [[00:44:00]](https://youtu.be/guZa7mQV1l0?t=2640) No retaliation; be "long-term greedy"

Threats and retaliation are nuclear strikes — toxic residue, and the watching crowd (the most important audience) judges you for it. No deal is better than a bad deal; Voss still regrets a "blood money" engagement that went bad over three years. Time is the one commodity everyone holds in equal measure — spend it on better opportunities, not bad debts. (Closing story: his mother's yardstick, and the terrorist-vs-Iowa-mom joke.)

## Books, Tools & Resources Mentioned

- ***Start With No*** — Jim Camp: making the other side feel free to say "no" respects their autonomy and relaxes them; also contains a phenomenally good chapter on open-ended questions.
- ***Thinking, Fast and Slow*** — Daniel Kahneman: the emotional component in every decision; groundwork for tactical empathy.
- ***Getting to Yes*** — Fisher & Ury: "phenomenally sound" but built on a drive to rationality that fails because people fear revealing their reasons.
- ***Beyond Winning*** — Robert Mnookin: a still-assigned chapter on the tension between empathy and assertiveness.
- ***The Black Swan*** — Nassim Taleb: the namesake — small innocuous things with massive impacts.
