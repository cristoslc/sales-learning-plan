# Positioning to Unlock Growth (with April Dunford)

- **Guest:** April Dunford, positioning expert and author of *Obviously Awesome*
- **Hosts:** Turing Fest (conference talk; YouTube copy of the "Positioning to Unlock Growth in Noisy Markets" session)
- **Podcast:** Turing Fest talk with April Dunford

## Key Takeaways

Positioning is the foundation everything else in marketing and sales is built on: it works by putting your product in a **market category**, which instantly triggers a powerful set of assumptions in buyers' minds about your competition, your features, who you sell to, and even your price ceiling. When those assumptions are correct, marketing and sales get a huge head start; when they are wrong, you lose before the demo. Dunford breaks positioning into five component pieces and gives a repeatable methodology: start with **competitive alternatives**, then derive your differentiated capabilities, the value they create, your best-fit customer segments, and finally the market category that makes that value obvious.

> 1. **Positioning defines how your product is the best in the world at delivering some value a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about** — everything in marketing and sales is the house; positioning is the foundation.
> 2. **Market category sets off assumptions** — competition, features, title of the person you sell to, and even an upper bound on price. Get the category right and the buyer assumes the rest; get it wrong and your product is "masquerading" as a weak version of something it isn't.
> 3. **The five components are interdependent** — Competitive Alternatives, Unique/Differentiated Capabilities, Differentiated Value, Target Customer Segments, and Market Category. You can't pick one without the others.
> 4. **Start with competitive alternatives** — "if you didn't exist, what would a customer do instead?" This is the stake in the ground; the most common failure is not knowing your *real* competitors (often a spreadsheet, an intern, or "do nothing").
> 5. **Positioning is not carved in stone** — it evolves deliberately (e.g., CRM for investment banks → banking → financial services), and a repositioning can transform a business.

## Guest Background

April Dunford is a positioning consultant, speaker, and author (the book she references is *Obviously Awesome*, which captures her positioning methodology, and she also hosts a podcast on positioning). She came to marketing from engineering school: straight out of engineering she was handed a poorly-selling product at a startup, stumbled through a repositioning that took off, and — after her boss quit — was made VP of Marketing with several more failing products to fix. She found no working methodology (even the academic "positioning statement" Mad Lib left her unable to pick the *best* category), and eventually reverse-engineered the pattern from her own repositionings.

## Core Thesis

Your product doesn't get to define itself to the market; buyers make sense of new things through **context**, and in marketing/sales the context is the **market category** you place yourself in. Positioning therefore is a deliberate act of choosing the category that (a) invokes correct assumptions, (b) is defensible against the alternatives your customer would otherwise choose, and (c) makes the value you alone can deliver obvious to the buyers who care most. Because the five components of positioning are interdependent, you can't wing it — you work them out in order, starting from the competitive alternative that is your "stake in the ground."

## Major Topics Discussed

### [[00:00:00]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=0) What positioning is (and isn't)

"Positioning defines how our product is the best in the world at delivering something, some value, that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about." Dunford clarifies what positioning is *not*: it's not messaging or a tagline, and it is *not* branding — branding and positioning are "completely separate," and positioning has to come first. The house analogy: "everything we do in marketing and sales is the house, positioning is the foundation upon which the house is built."

### [[00:02:00]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=120) Context, market categories, and the assumptions they trigger

New products are understood through context. Dunford's Crocs gag and noodle-cone examples make the point that context changes how you perceive a product entirely. In business, the context-setter is the **market category** — the 8,000-company martech landscape is only navigable because it's split into macro and micro categories. And the category you pick sets off a chain of assumptions: in her "CRM" pitch-contest example, saying "it's a CRM" instantly tells buyers your competition is Salesforce, what features to expect, who buys it, *and* establishes an upper bound on your price. "If I position myself in a market category I have established an upper bound for pricing."

### [[00:09:10]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=550) The cost of wrong positioning: "email for lawyers"

A VC-backed startup was "email for lawyers" — an "amazing product with amazing technology that customers love, masquerading as a kind of crappy email that doesn't even have a calendar." Positioned as email, it competed with Gmail (and couldn't win on the free-email price anchor) and was judged by email feature expectations. Simply recontextualizing the identical product as **"team collaboration for lawyers"** changed who they competed with (Slack, Microsoft Teams), removed the calendar expectation, highlighted the real capability (secure, context-aware file sharing), and justified a much higher price.

### [[00:13:08]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=788) Why products get positioned by accident: ClearPath Robotics

Most positioning is not deliberate — products are launched as "better email" or "a robot," customers react, and a confusing category sticks. ClearPath Robotics built a sophisticated robot for manufacturing but "the minute we said that word robot," the buyer compared them to their 10 years of approved robot vendors and their price expectations. Repositioning the *same* machine as **"autonomous vehicles for industrial uses"** reframed the conversation entirely — of course it drives around and is full of mapping/sensors/AI, and of course it costs more than a pick-and-place robot. The product even has headlights and brake lights purely to reinforce "this is like a little car."

### [[00:17:00]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=1020) The failure of the "positioning statement" and the birth of a methodology

Dunford found nobody actually had a method: the classic **positioning statement** (a Mad-Libs "We are a ___ that does ___ unlike ___") couldn't tell her how to pick the *best* market category — "trust me, you'll just know." So she broke positioning into its component pieces and solved each. Her engineering instinct: decompose, solve the parts, then assemble.

### [[00:22:02]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=1322) The five components of positioning

1. **Competitive Alternatives** — if you didn't exist, what would a customer do instead? What you must position against.
2. **Unique attributes / capabilities / features** — things you can do that your competition can't.
3. **Differentiated value** — the value your features enable for the customer's business that no one else can deliver ("nobody cares what your features are; they care what they enable").
4. **Target customer segments** — best-fit customers who really care a lot about the value only you can deliver.
5. **Market category** — the context (robot vs. self-driving car; email vs. team collaboration).

She stresses they're all interdependent: differentiated value depends on your unique capabilities; those are only unique vs. a competitor; best-fit customers are defined by caring about your unique value; and the best market category makes your value obvious to them.

### [[00:25:30]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=1530) The ordering insight (jobs-to-be-done) — start with competitive alternatives

After a frustrating two-year cycle of guessing-and-testing, Clayton Christensen's **jobs-to-be-done** thinking produced the epiphany that you must **start with competitive alternatives**. If you don't, you get positioning that "sounds good in the office" but is "insufficiently differentiated" from what the customer would otherwise do. The sequence: competitive alternatives as your stake in the ground → your differentiated capabilities → the value each delivers → the characteristics of accounts that care most → the market category that makes the value clear.

### [[00:27:30]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=1650) The most common way to screw it up: wrong competitors

Dunford's favorite caution: startups name the two-guys-in-a-basement startups as competition and win on "ease of use" — but admit they never lose to those companies; they lose to **"no decision"** while the customer solves the problem with "a spreadsheet" or "an intern." Your real competitive alternative is often the workaround, and you must understand it first ("are you going to beat the intern on ease of use? No."). Positioning has to be built against whoever the customer would *actually* choose instead.

### [[00:29:01]](https://youtu.be/yTQaiForo6k?t=1741) The Siebel story: CRM → CRM for investment banks → $1.7B exit

As VP of Marketing at a small enterprise-CRM underdog positioned against Siebel ("in no way better than them"), she lost every head-to-head. A lucky sales rep got a meeting at Goldman Sachs, and suddenly the differentiator (unusually flexible relationship modeling) was exactly what excited investment bankers. Repositioning as **"CRM for investment banks"** was transformitive: they never faced Siebel head-to-head again (bankers saw them as purpose-built for investment banking), investment banks started calling them, and the company went from ~$2M to ~$80M revenue in ~18 months — before Siebel acquired them for **$1.7 billion**. The team had to deliberately argue that positioning is evolutionary: win investment banking first, then retail banking, then insurance (CRM for financial services), then take on the general-purpose leader — a deliberate ladder, not a random one.
