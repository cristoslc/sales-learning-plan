# Startup Sales Maturity Model & Founder-Led Selling — Pete Kazanjy

- **Speaker:** Pete Kazanjy, co-founder of TalentBin (acquired by Monster), Atrium, and Modern Sales Pros; author of *Founding Sales*
- **Event:** Startup sales panel with David Sacks and Stephanie (Xamarin) — Kazanjy covers the founder-led selling stages
- **Format:** Conference Talk

## Key Takeaways

Founder-led selling is not a temporary embarrassment to be skipped — it is the mechanism by which the sales motion gets debugged before anyone else has to run it. Kazanjy, a product-marketing founder who became his company's first rep, then sales manager, then VP of Sales, maps the stages and their entry/exit criteria.

> 1. **Founders' antipathy for sales is a pattern to overcome, not a personality trait** — most founders have no sales background, so their mental model of selling comes from TV. Meanwhile the "we don't do sales" companies they admire (Twilio, Stripe, Slack, Dropbox, Atlassian, Palantir) are "filthy with salespeople" [~00:02:00].
> 2. **Beware the anti-patterns** — "let's sprinkle some sales on it" (hiring a sales team before the motion exists), and "revenue swaps" with fellow accelerator companies instead of selling to real, unfriendly customers [~00:04:00].
> 3. **The founder must bake the sales motion first** — traditional sellers execute existing processes; they don't have the skills (or the 24 months of subject-matter expertise) to debug and *create* one. "You can't get the scale until you've killed the first couples" of sales reps who joined too early.
> 4. **Work the stage you're in, not the stage you wish you were at** — each stage has entry criteria and exit criteria; jumping ahead before hitting the exit criteria "can have disastrous consequences" (his 18-months-later reminder about a founder who dismissed cold email: the company was dead).
> 5. **Customer development before building: not three interviews** — Atrium ran ~150 in half a year; a dozen to two dozen starts showing patterns. The business founder leads, and the goal is to back-check hypotheses about the problem and the willingness to pay [~00:07:00].
> 6. **Beta customers prove the KPIs, not revenue** — deploy with a dozen users to demonstrate the product solves the validated problem (e.g., Xamarin's 30–45% reduction in cross-platform mobile development time). The interviewees who said "I'd pay for this" are your back pocket for this stage [~00:09:00].
> 7. **"Will someone pay?" is a nonzero test, not a pricing-optimization exercise** — prove anyone will pay, instrument that they actually capture the promised value (TalentBin's miss: great at selling via demo, lax on value capture), and price off muscular ROI math ("a mobile software engineer costs $150K/yr; 45% of that saved is a lot of money") [~00:11:00].
> 8. **Repetition is the feature, not the bug** — the same conversation 3–5 times a day is how you find corner cases and objections; each one becomes either a product requirement or a documented objection handler [~00:12:00].
> 9. **Get leverage through abstraction** — hire an SDR (or use a "robot SDR" service) so the founder spends time selling and succeeding, not prospecting; or hire CS help so closed customers get handed off cleanly [~00:13:00].
> 10. **The end state is a "revenue mitochondrion"** — SDR → AE → CSM running tightly, cloned as many times as possible. The founder's exit from day-to-day selling is complete when non-founders sell at least as well as the founder did [~00:15:00].

## Guest Background

Pete Kazanjy describes himself as "a business generalist founder that then became the first rep, Sales Manager, eventually our VP quote-unquote VP of Sales" at TalentBin, which was acquired by Monster Worldwide with about 20 SDRs and AEs. After the acquisition, he led new product sales for 600 old-school reps at Monster, wrote *Founding Sales* (at the time of this talk, "a Google Doc floating around that people pass on to each other"), founded sales-analytics company Atrium, and runs Modern Sales Pros, "the canonical sales operations, enablement and leadership community."

## Core Thesis

The stages of go-to-market form a ladder: each stage's exit criteria unlock the next, and the value unlocked compounds at the abstraction stages — because a business that only the founder can sell is capped at the founder's 40–60 hours a week, while a documented, trainable sales motion can be instantiated in other people's brains and parallelized. Skipping stages ("sprinkling some sales on it") produces the classic failures: sales hires who can't replicate the founder's hit rate, and companies whose go-to-market never became intentional. The founder's irreplaceability at the start is precisely why they must do the selling first — and their job becomes enabling others, then building the system, in sequence.

## Major Topics Discussed

### [[00:00:30]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=30) Why founders avoid sales — and why the excuse doesn't survive contact with the evidence

70% of founders in the room had no sales background; their experience of "sales" is what they've seen on TV. The anti-pattern stories ("product-led growth means no sales") collapse on inspection: the admired product-led companies employ salespeople at scale. A seductive related anti-pattern: doing "revenue swaps" with accelerator peers instead of selling to real prospects — which proves nothing about the market.

### [[00:05:30]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=330) The maturity model: grind through each stage

The stages map to the classic customer-development ladder: (1) do we know what problem we're solving? → (2) does the thing actually work? → (3) will someone pay for it? → (4) will many pay? → (5) can non-founders sell it? → (6) can a managed team sell it? Each stage's exit criteria gate the next. Later stages unlock "discontinuous value creation" because B2B businesses scale by adding quota-bearing reps doing parallelized revenue acquisition.

### [[00:07:00]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=420) Stage 1 — Customer development

Engage potential customers to back-check hypotheses about the problem and the solution. At Atrium they ran ~150 interviews in half a year — but patterns emerge after a dozen or two. Do it before building, with both business and technical founders; the business founder typically leads. Anti-pattern: building in a cave and bouncing off the market ("build something people don't want" — Paul Graham). Resource: First Round Review's "Getting in the Van" (Michael Sippy).

### [[00:09:00]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=540) Stage 2 — Beta customers prove the KPIs, not revenue

Recruit a dozen beta deployments purely to demonstrate the product solves the problem against explicit KPIs (the Xamarin example: reduce cross-platform mobile development time by a stated percentage). Practical tip: during customer development, stockpile the "smart and excited" interviewees in your CRM and customer advisory panel — then return to them at this stage: "you said you'd pay this amount... I took notes."

### [[00:10:30]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=630) Stage 3 — Will someone pay (nonzero)

Pricing here is ROI-rationale pricing, not optimization. The goal is proof of willingness to pay plus proof of value capture — customers must get the promised value out (the TalentBin miss). The "ask for money" discomfort is real and the answer is practice; muscular ROI metrics make the ask feel natural. Still founder-executed: this is the trial-and-error baking of the initial sales motion.

### [[00:12:00]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=720) Stage 4 — Will many pay: small-scale repeatability

The pleasant misery of the same prospect conversation 3–5 times daily is the mechanism: it surfaces every objection. Each objection gets a response (from the subject-matter expert), a product requirement, or a documented objection handler — this documentation is the sales motion taking shape. Exit criteria: one-to-two dozen customers and "the beginnings of a documented sales motion." Leverage options at this stage: an SDR for prospecting, CS help for post-close handoff, or (his Atrium pattern) founder-led meetings plus CS because his network generates meetings.

### [[00:14:00]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=840) Stage 5 — Can non-founders sell it

The fork: hire AEs and pour the founder's brain into theirs (the preferred path with a business founder who can manage while technical co-founders mind the store), or — for solo founders past the point where their own selling time is the bottleneck — hire management earlier. New muscles required: hiring, training, documentation, mock demos, call listening, coaching. Exit criteria: the AEs sell at least as well as the founder — which is only knowable because the founder established what "good" looks like in the prior stage.

### [[00:15:30]](https://youtu.be/lAutzV21Gus?t=930) Stage 6 — The revenue mitochondrion

An evolved org where the founder is fully out of day-to-day selling and instead manages the cohesion of SDR → AE → CSM as a clonable unit of revenue production. Anti-pattern: hiring the sales team before the motion is proven — unless you find the rare "early-stage evangelical sales leader... a weaponized product manager, product marketer," which is usually a business co-founder anyway.
